Cruciate Ligament Surgery (TPLO) for Dogs

Cranial cruciate ligament rupture is the most common cause of hind-leg lameness in dogs. TPLO is one of the most common orthopedic procedures we perform, and most dogs go home the same day or the day after.

TPLO at a glance

Getting your dog back on four legs

The cranial cruciate ligament (CCL) stabilizes the knee, called the stifle in dogs. When it tears, the shin bone slides forward with every step, causing pain, lameness and, over time, arthritis.

Unlike in people, the ligament is not replaced. Instead, surgery changes the mechanics of the knee so it stays stable without it. The technique depends on your dog's breed, weight, age, the severity of the injury and the shape of the tibia. For most dogs we recommend TPLO.

TPLO is one of several orthopedic procedures we perform as part of our veterinary surgery services in Santa Monica.

Signs and symptoms

Signs of a torn cruciate ligament

Lameness in a back leg is the main sign. It can start suddenly or build up gradually, and range from a mild limp to barely putting weight on the leg. How it shows depends on the type of injury:

  1. 01

    Partial rupture

    Typically seen in young, large-breed dogs. Lameness can be mild at first but gets worse as the ligament degenerates, and the ligament usually goes on to rupture completely.

  2. 02

    Complete rupture

    Usually seen in middle-aged dogs, often during normal exercise. Lameness is marked; it may improve at first, but dogs typically keep a limp.

  3. 03

    Traumatic rupture

    Follows an accident, such as a car accident or a leg caught while jumping a fence. Lameness is sudden and severe.

Treatment

Does my dog really need surgery?

The most common cause is progressive degeneration of the ligament. Abnormal conformation and gait, the shape of the tibia, obesity and lack of fitness have been suggested as causes, but none has been proven.

For most dogs, surgery is the only way to return to good function. Braces, medication, supplements or physical therapy alone will not stabilize the knee.

The procedure

How TPLO works

TPLO stands for tibial plateau leveling osteotomy. It changes the angle at the upper end of the tibia so the knee no longer depends on the torn ligament.

  1. 1

    Measure

    X-rays of the stifle (knee) are taken before surgery to measure the angle at the upper end of the shin bone, the tibial plateau angle.

  2. 2

    Inspect the joint

    The inside of the joint is explored to check the menisci. Damaged cartilage is removed so your dog can regain normal, pain-free function.

  3. 3

    Level the plateau

    A curved cut is made on the inner side of the upper end of the tibia, and the upper portion is rotated to a post-surgical angle of about 4 to 10°, similar to the human knee.

  4. 4

    Stabilize

    A locking bone plate and screws hold the bone in its new position while it heals, eliminating the instability known as cranial tibial thrust.

Aftercare

Recovery after TPLO

Aftercare is very important. Keep your dog on a leash or harness and avoid running, jumping, stairs and play until we tell you otherwise.

Physical therapy at our hospital can support the controlled-exercise weeks, and our team will tell you when to start.

  1. Day 0 – 1

    Most dogs go home the same day or the next, with pain medication and antibiotics. A cone may be needed if your dog licks the wound.

  2. Weeks 1 – 2

    Recheck to look at the wound and remove sutures. Leash-only walks for toileting, and crate or small-room rest the rest of the time.

  3. Weeks 3 – 6

    Exercise increases gradually and in a controlled way, always on a leash. Physical therapy may be recommended.

  4. Weeks 6 – 8

    Check-up with x-rays to confirm the bone cut is healing, and advice on how to increase exercise.

  5. Months 2 – 3

    Rehabilitation is completed and your dog returns to normal activity as advised.

Techniques

TPLO, TTA and lateral suture compared

All three aim to stabilize the knee. TPLO and TTA reshape the tibia; the lateral suture does not. We also offer CBLO (CORA-based leveling osteotomy) for selected cases.

About TTA

TTA (tibial tuberosity advancement) moves the attachment of the patellar tendon forward to offset the lost ligament. Dr. Pedraza uses titanium plates and places bone graft to speed healing. Recovery takes 6 to 8 weeks, with x-rays at 6 weeks.

Why we no longer do lateral suture

The lateral (extracapsular) suture is a traditional technique that relies on scar tissue forming around the joint. The suture stretches and can break over time, especially in heavier dogs, so we recommend TPLO or TTA.

Questions

TPLO surgery FAQ

Still have questions? Call (310) 310-3704.

How long does recovery from TPLO surgery take?

Rehabilitation usually takes 2 to 3 months. Exercise is strictly limited for the first few weeks and is then increased gradually on a leash, with x-rays at 6 to 8 weeks to confirm that the bone is healing.

Will my dog have to stay in the hospital?

Most dogs go home the same day of surgery or the day after, with pain medication, antibiotics and clear aftercare instructions. Overnight monitoring is available on select days for dogs that need extended post-operative care.

Is TPLO better than TTA?

Both reshape the tibia so the knee stays stable without the ligament. TPLO levels the tibial plateau; TTA moves the attachment of the patellar tendon forward. The right choice depends on your dog's breed, weight, age, the severity of the injury and the shape of the tibia, and we recommend one after examining your dog and reviewing x-rays.

Do you still perform lateral suture (extracapsular) repair?

No. Lateral suture is an older technique: the suture is a temporary stabilizer that stretches and can break over time, especially in heavier dogs. We recommend procedures that change the shape of the bone, such as TPLO.

Can a torn cruciate ligament heal without surgery?

For most dogs, no. Braces, medication, supplements or physical therapy alone do not stabilize the knee, and surgery is usually the only way to return to good function.

Do you accept referrals for TPLO from other veterinarians?

What are the signs of a torn cruciate ligament in dogs?

Lameness in a back leg is the main sign. It can start suddenly or build gradually, and may show only after rest. Dogs may be slow to rise, reluctant to jump into the car, stiff, or less keen to play. A partial tear often worsens until the ligament ruptures completely.

Signs and symptoms →

How is a cruciate ligament tear diagnosed?

Often the abnormal motion of the knee can be felt during a hands-on exam, though partial tears are subtler. Mild sedation and x-rays may be needed. Before surgery we take x-rays in-house to measure the tibial plateau angle and plan the procedure.

Can my dog tear the cruciate ligament in the other knee?

Yes. Up to about half of dogs with a cruciate tear in one knee develop the same problem in the other, usually within 12 to 18 months, according to the sources listed below. Keeping a healthy weight and regular, moderate activity are the factors owners can influence.

How is pain controlled during and after TPLO?

We use an epidural and Nocita®, a long-acting local anesthetic, so the surgical area stays comfortable after your dog wakes up. Most dogs go home the same day or the next with pain medication and antibiotics, and we tell you exactly how to give them.

What can my dog do at home after TPLO?

Keep your dog on a leash or harness for toileting, with no running, jumping, stairs or play until we say so, and rest in a crate or small room. Exercise then increases gradually, and physical therapy may be recommended.

How well do dogs do after cruciate ligament surgery?

Most dogs return to an active lifestyle once the bone has healed. Veterinary orthopedic guidance reports significant long-term improvement in about 85 to 90% of dogs treated surgically, though results depend on your dog, the technique and how closely aftercare is followed.
