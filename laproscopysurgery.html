At a glance

Precision, safety and a faster recovery

Laparoscopy lets us operate through tiny incisions with a camera and specialized instruments. Compared with traditional surgery it means less pain, minimal scarring and faster healing, a level of care once reserved for people.

Our laparoscopic services are led by Dr. Antonio Pedraza. To offer the most focused surgical care, we have also brought our laparoscopic techniques together at our dedicated facility, Los Angeles Laparoscopy Vet.

Laparoscopic spay vs traditional spay

Why choose a laparoscopic spay?

Both remove the ovaries. The difference is how much your dog goes through to get there.

Comparison of laparoscopic and traditional spay
CriterionLaparoscopic spayTraditional spay
IncisionsOne or two tiny incisions, about the size of a dimeOne larger abdominal incision
Ovarian ligamentCarefully cut and cauterized, with virtually no bleedingTorn from the body, causing pain and bruising
Pain after surgeryUp to 65% less post-operative painMore significant

AnesthesiaShorterLonger
RecoveryUsually 5 to 7 daysUsually 14 to 21 days of limited activity

Laparoscopy spay

The laparoscopic spay, step by step

It starts with a consultation about your dog's health and timing. Because spaying is elective, we plan it for the right moment: usually after the first heat cycle, 2 to 3 weeks after it has fully finished.

Before surgery we perform a physical exam and pre-anesthetic bloodwork. Your dog is anesthetized and monitored throughout, two small incisions let us remove the ovaries with minimal trauma, and we check the entire abdomen for any abnormalities.

Recovery usually takes 5 to 7 days. We use intradermal sutures, so there is nothing to remove; an e-collar or recovery bodysuit protects the incisions, and we send home every medication your dog needs.

Laparoscopy gastropexy

Preventing bloat before it happens

Gastric dilatation-volvulus (GDV), or bloat, is a life-threatening emergency in which the stomach twists. A preventive gastropexy permanently attaches the stomach to the body wall so it cannot twist.

It is recommended for large, deep-chested breeds at risk, such as Great Danes, Irish Wolfhounds, German Shepherds and Standard Poodles, and it can be done at the same time as a spay or cryptorchid neuter, so your dog only needs one anesthetic.

Afterwards, the small incisions are closed with sutures that are removed in about 14 days. Feed smaller, more frequent meals and keep your dog calm, with no running, jumping or rough play, for the first couple of weeks.

  • Incisions about the size of a dime
  • Recovery in days instead of 2 to 3 weeks
  • Can be combined with a spay or neuter

More laparoscopic procedures

Cryptorchid neuter and laparoscopic biopsy

  • Laparoscopy cryptorchid neuter

    Cryptorchidism is when one or both testicles do not descend into the scrotum, which raises the risk of testicular tumors and hormonal problems. A high-definition camera with up to 10x magnification lets Dr. Pedraza find and remove the retained testicle through two dime-sized incisions.

    We do not perform this procedure with traditional open surgery because of the extra tissue trauma. Most dogs are back to normal activity in 5 to 7 days.

  • Laparoscopy biopsy

    We take tissue samples, most commonly from the liver, in dogs and cats with unexplained liver, spleen or other abdominal problems, or to investigate inflammatory, infectious or cancerous conditions.

    Small incisions mean less pain, less bleeding and a faster recovery; most pets go home the same day. Samples go to a laboratory, and we work with your referring veterinarian on the diagnosis.

Laparoscopy FAQ

Still have questions? Call (310) 310-3704.

What is a laparoscopic spay?

A minimally invasive spay in which a small camera and instruments are inserted through one or two tiny incisions to remove the ovaries, instead of making one larger abdominal incision.

What are the benefits of a laparoscopic spay over a traditional spay?

Less pain (up to 65% less post-operative pain), minimal scarring, shorter anesthesia, a magnified view of the abdomen and a faster recovery of about 5 to 7 days instead of 14 to 21.

When should I spay my dog?

We usually recommend waiting until after the first heat cycle, typically 2 to 3 weeks after it has fully finished.

Do the stitches need to be removed?

Not after a laparoscopic spay or cryptorchid neuter, which are closed with intradermal sutures or surgical glue. After a gastropexy, sutures are removed in about 14 days.

Which dogs should have a gastropexy?

Large, deep-chested breeds at risk of bloat, such as Great Danes, Irish Wolfhounds, German Shepherds and Standard Poodles. It can be combined with a spay or cryptorchid neuter.

What is a cryptorchid neuter?

The removal of one or both testicles that did not descend into the scrotum. We perform it laparoscopically, which means smaller incisions, less pain and a faster recovery.
